So while working on this, I ended up finding out something pretty interesting that deserved a closer look. I needed a chat model that would lose some of its refusals, but not all of them, so that we could test ways to repair it. Here’s how it went:

  1. The first try lost nearly all of them.

  2. The next three tries (a gentler recipe, a bigger model and a smaller adapter) were not much better.

  3. A different model lost about half.

Even though I wasn’t able to get what I wanted (at that point), I did happen to find something a bit more interesting.

Some background first. A chat model like these has already been trained to refuse harmful requests. People then train it a little more on their own data, which is usually ordinary: customer questions, summaries, product help. This is called fine-tuning, and earlier work (Fine-tuning Aligned Language Models Compromises Safety, Even When Users Do Not Intend To!, Qi et al.) found that it can wear away the refusals, even when the new data contains nothing harmful. Our data was 4,800 everyday instructions and answers from a public dataset called Dolly. Each model got one short pass over it, with exactly the same settings.

Before training, all three models were mostly careful: between 3% and 14% of their answers to 200 harmful requests were judged unsafe by a safety classifier. Afterwards, OLMo-2 was at about 48% (three training runs). Qwen2.5 went from 5% to 80%, and Llama-3.2 from 14% to 76% (one run each).

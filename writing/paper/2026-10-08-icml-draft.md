## Abstract

Fine-tuning a safety-tuned language model on benign instruction data can erode its refusals of harmful requests. When only the training data may change, the owner must choose a repair: remove some rows, or add safety data. Many repairs have been proposed, but they have rarely been compared on equal terms, with training seeds, side effects and their compute counted. We measure which data repair restores the most safety for the compute spent without raising over-refusal or losing task skill. After benign fine-tuning of OLMo-2-1B-Instruct with a fixed LoRA recipe that erodes it partly, six pre-registered repairs, each run with six training seeds, are compared with random removal of the same size and with no repair: removal of rows chosen by training-data attribution, by a representation filter or by a free answer-length rule, and addition of random, selected or self-generated refusal examples. Each repair is labelled better, equivalent within 5 percentage points, worse, or inconclusive, and reported with its over-refusal, task loss and measured cost. Five references that change no data set the bar that a data repair must clear: a safety prompt, a down-scaled adapter, a post-hoc projection of the adapter, a gentler learning-rate schedule, and a published objective that constrains updates on the first answer tokens. A second family, Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, is planned at a matched erosion dose. Pre-registered gates test whether the erosion is localized in data categories, a positive control tests whether diagnostics find a planted cause [PLACEHOLDER: if funded], and model-based predictions of when diagnosis pays are written down in advance and scored on the second family. ## 1 Introduction

Fine-tuning a safety-tuned ("Instruct") language model on data that contains nothing harmful can still erode its refusals of harmful requests. Qi et al. (2024) observed this after one epoch on ordinary instruction data sets (Alpaca, Dolly) for Llama-2-7b-Chat and GPT-3.5 Turbo; He et al. (2024) showed that benign rows selected for their similarity to harmful examples erode safety far more than random rows; and Guo et al. (2026) report that 100 random benign rows of full fine-tuning raise attack success sharply on recent 8B-class Instruct models. The size of the effect depends on the recipe (Kim et al., 2025). When a fine-tune does erode safety, its owner often controls only the data: commercial fine-tuning services screen training data, gate the tuned model on a safety evaluation, and report harm categories rather than rows, leaving the customer to fix the data and resubmit (Microsoft, 2026).

Repair is then a data decision with side effects. One can *remove* rows: published filters score benign rows by their contribution to erosion (He et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026b), and training-data attribution ranks rows by gradient similarity to harmful examples (Jiao et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2026). One can *add* safety data, but which data are added decides the outcome: on Llama3-8B-Instruct, averaged over seven downstream tasks, a random 1% mix of safety rows raised attack success from 20.24% (no safety data) to 59.23%, while DataRx's selected 1% mix lowered it to 13.70% (Zhang et al., 2026a). Safety data can also cause over-refusal (Bianchi et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2026b). And some repairs need no data change at all: a safety prompt used only at test time (Lyu et al., 2024), a gentler recipe (Kim et al., 2025), a post-hoc projection of the fine-tuned adapter (Hsu et al., 2024), or a fine-tuning objective that constrains updates on the first answer tokens (Qi et al., 2025). Every data repair costs compute, and the only direct evidence about it is a retrain.

Which repair works best is an empirical question that published results do not settle. Each repair was evaluated in its own setting, with its own models, judges and baselines, mostly with one or a few training seeds, and seldom with its scoring compute charged against the alternative of retraining [CITE-CHECK]. The closest comparison we found, DualSelect (Chen et al., 2026), compares several selection methods with three seeds and a false-refusal rate [CITE-CHECK], but not addition repairs, model-level references or measured cost. Three results suggest that expensive diagnosis may not pay. Once selection compute is charged, powerful data-selection methods are almost never compute-optimal (Yin & Rush, 2025) [CITE-CHECK]. Gradient-based attribution tracks the answer format of training examples more than their task (Kim et al., 2026) [CITE-CHECK], so a free format or length rule may match it. And the erosion may be diffuse: even subsets selected for low conflict with safety still erode it (Guo et al., 2026), and Springer et al. (2026) argue from theory that better curation cannot remove the fragility.

We therefore measure repairs directly (§3, §4). On OLMo-2-1B-Instruct fine-tuned on 4,800 Dolly rows with a fixed LoRA recipe (recipe B: a constant learning rate, one epoch), six repairs fixed in advance are each run over six training seeds and compared with random removal of the same size and with no repair: three removals of 600 rows (ranked by training-data attribution, by a representation filter in the style of LARF, and by answer length) and three additions of 48 refusal examples (drawn at random, selected in the style of DataRx, or written by the untuned model itself). Each contrast is labelled *better*, *equivalent* (its 90% interval lies within ±5 percentage points, pp), *worse* (significantly harmful on the unsafe rate, over-refusal or task loss) or *inconclusive*, after Holm correction. Over-refusal, measured against the untuned model, and task loss on held-out rows are feasibility limits. Cost is measured in GPU-seconds and in full-run equivalents (FRE) under two conventions. A data repair counts as dominant only if it beats five references that change no data, none of them ours: a safety prompt at test time, the fine-tuned adapter scaled down, its Safe LoRA projection (Hsu et al., 2024), recipe G (a recipe-only fix: warmup and cosine decay, which halve the mean learning rate), and recipe B with the constrained objective of Qi et al. (2025). A second family, Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, is planned with recipe B at its own learning rate, chosen from erosion probes alone so that both families erode partly (a matched erosion dose; §4.1).

Gate C asks which diagnostic picks the culprit category, and whether a format rule explains attribution's pick. A positive control plants a known eroding cause in one cell of nested menus of 8, 32 and 128 cells [TO BE FINALISED] and asks whether each diagnostic finds it, and at which menu size diagnosis beats trial runs [PLACEHOLDER: if funded]. A secondary, model-based analysis predicts when paying for a diagnostic beats spending the same compute on trial runs selected by successive halving (Jamieson & Talwalkar, 2016; Li et al., 2018); its predictions are written down before Gate B's category runs and scored on the second family. ## 2 Related Work

**Safety erosion under benign fine-tuning.** Qi et al. (2024) showed that fine-tuning aligned models on benign instruction data degrades safety, though less than harmful data does, with no training seeds reported. He et al. (2024) selected benign rows by representation and gradient similarity to harmful anchors; in their best case, 100 such rows gave over 70% unsafe answers against under 20% for random rows. This shows that benign rows *can* erode safety, not that ordinary fine-tunes have a localized cause. Kim et al. (2025) change the size of the erosion several-fold through optimization choices alone. Guo et al. (2026) find that 100 random benign rows of full fine-tuning erode recent 8B-class Instruct models, and that a handful of safety rows restores safety after a small attack. Under LoRA their results depend on the family: in their Table 2, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct reads 7.27% before fine-tuning, 4.50% after 100 rows and 9.09% after 5,000 rows (two seeds), while Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct rises to 44.80% at 5,000 rows. Springer et al. (2026) explain erosion geometrically, as benign updates curving into a sharp, safety-relevant subspace.

**Removal repairs: filters and attribution.** Published filters score benign rows for their contribution to erosion. DataShield projects rows onto a compliance direction (Zhang et al., 2026b); LARF uses representations of safety-sensitive layers (Li et al., 2025); SQSD projects each row's parameter update onto danger and safety directions (Wang et al., 2026b); bi-anchoring compares a row's gradients or representations with harmful and safe anchors (He et al., 2024); and Self-Inf-N, proposed as an attack, finds outlier benign rows whose fine-tuning breaks safety (Guan et al., 2025) [CITE-CHECK]. Row filters report their scoring cost without charging it against runs (Zhang et al., 2026b; Hu et al., 2026) [CITE-CHECK]; other defences select tokens (Li et al., 2026) or use continual learning (Alssum et al., 2025) [CITE-CHECK]. DualSelect (Chen et al., 2026) selects task rows and safety references jointly and compares it with filters including LARF, TOSS and SQSD [CITE-CHECK]. For attribution, Pan et al. (2026) find that removing rows flagged by gradient-based attribution gives a safer retrained model than removing rows flagged by WildGuard, from one training run per arm and with no compute reported. DATE-LM (Jiao et al., 2025) finds that with safety-aligned look-alikes in the data, attribution's detection drops while LLM classifiers are largely unaffected; without them, the strongest classifiers detect better, yet removing attribution's picks gives the safer retrained model. Luo et al. (2026b) report weak, inconsistent effects from deleting or reweighting influential rows, large effects from rewriting them, and an over-refusal trade-off for safety rewriting.

**Addition and model-level repairs.** Adding about 3% safety examples to instruction tuning improves safety but can cause exaggerated refusals (Bianchi et al., 2024) [CITE-CHECK]. Which rows are added matters: DataRx selects safety rows by the gap between a model's own response and a safe reference (Zhang et al., 2026a) [CITE-CHECK], and its random mix made the model less safe than no safety data, while its selected mix made it safer (§1). Guo et al. (2026) restore safety with a handful of safety rows, and Llama 3's safety mix was tuned by repeated experiments that traded violations against false refusals (Grattafiori et al., 2024). Model-level repairs change no data: a safety prompt used only at test time reduces erosion (PTST; Lyu et al., 2024), SafeTune compares repairs of safety drift in single deterministic runs (Seth et al., 2026) [CITE-CHECK], a gentler recipe shrinks erosion (Kim et al., 2025), Safe LoRA projects the LoRA weights of selected layers onto a safety-aligned subspace without training or data (Hsu et al., 2024), and an objective that constrains updates on the first answer tokens makes alignment more persistent under fine-tuning (Qi et al., 2025). We use such repairs as references that a data repair must beat.

**Attribution, its cost and what it tracks.** Training-data attribution ranges from cheap gradient similarity on LoRA gradients (LESS; Xia et al., 2024) [CITE-CHECK] to estimates of finite removal effects by unrolled differentiation (SOURCE; Bae et al., 2024), which are reported to cost several training runs [CITE-CHECK]. Xiao & Aranguri (2026) compare the cost of diagnosis methods for undesirable post-training behaviours; probe attribution is over ten times cheaper than gradient attribution and LLM-judge baselines in their study. Yin & Rush (2025) charge selection compute against training compute and find that powerful selection methods are almost never compute-optimal; gradient-based selection pays only when the trained model is about ten times larger than the selection model [CITE-CHECK]. Kim et al. (2026) find that gradient similarity tracks answer format more than task [CITE-CHECK]; we therefore include a free answer-length rule as a repair and as a control for attribution's picks.

**Predicting outcomes and side effects.** Persona vectors (Chen et al., 2025) project training data onto activation directions to flag data that will shift a trait [CITE-CHECK]. TuneAhead (Luo et al., 2026a) and Data2Behavior (Wang et al., 2026a) predict a fine-tune's outcome from its data before training, and MNEME predicts side effects of fine-tuning and unlearning by sparse model diffing (Kassem et al., 2025) [CITE-CHECK]. Any such predictor can enter our comparison as a scorer at its measured cost; side-effect prediction is a secondary analysis here (§4.10).

**Decisions under a compute budget.** Successive halving and Hyperband allocate a fixed budget across candidates by repeatedly discarding the worst at increasing fidelity (Jamieson & Talwalkar, 2016; Li et al., 2018); multi-fidelity methods model how cheap observations relate to full ones (e.g. Ouyang et al., 2025). DataDecide (Magnusson et al., 2025) scores small pretraining runs by decision accuracy against compute, and Ivison et al. (2025) charge selection compute against training on more data [CITE-CHECK].

**How we differ.** We do not claim a first benchmark of safety filters, a first comparison of removal against addition, or a first pricing of data selection: DualSelect, DataShield's cost table and Yin & Rush (2025) each cover part of this ground [CITE-CHECK]. Our contribution is the combination; to our knowledge, and not found by our search, no prior work combines equivalence-bounded contrasts over six training seeds for removal and addition repairs; over-refusal and task loss as limits; measured costs under two conventions; model-level references that a data repair must beat; a positive control with a planted cause; and predictions about the value of diagnosis written down before the data.

## 3 Problem Setup

### 3.1 Setting and endpoints

Let $\theta_0$ be a safety-tuned Instruct model, fine-tuned with a fixed recipe $\mathcal{R}$ (adapter, loss, optimizer, schedule, length rule) on benign data $\mathcal{D} = D_1 \cup \dots \cup D_K$, split into $K$ *units* by metadata fixed before any outcome. A *repair* $a$ maps $(\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{R})$ to a training set and recipe $(a(\mathcal{D}), \mathcal{R}_a)$; a data repair keeps $\mathcal{R}_a = \mathcal{R}$. The model trained with seed $s$ (adapter initialization and data order) is $\theta(a,s)$. For a model $\theta$, $f(\theta)$ is the *unsafe rate* (the share of sampled answers to a harm bank that a frozen judge labels unsafe), $\ell(\theta)$ is the held-out *task loss*, and $r(\theta)$ is the *over-refusal rate* on benign prompts that resemble harmful ones. Endpoints average over seeds: $F(a) = \mathbb{E}_s[f(\theta(a,s))]$, and likewise $\bar\ell(a)$ and $R(a)$. With $a_0$ the fine-tune without repair, the *erosion*, refusal erosion as Qwen3Guard labels it (§4.2), is $E = F(a_0) - f(\theta_0)$; repair is meaningful only when $E$ exceeds seed noise (Gate A, §4.6).

### 3.2 Repair contrasts, limits and labels (primary)

Let $a_{\mathrm{R}}$ remove the same number of random rows as the removal repairs. A removal repair $m$ is compared with random removal, $\Lambda_m = F(a_{\mathrm{R}}) - F(m)$: the safety it restores beyond removing the same amount of data at random. An addition repair is compared with no repair, $\Lambda_m = F(a_0) - F(m)$, and a selected or self-generated addition with the random addition of the same size. The restoration $G_m = F(a_0) - F(m)$ is reported for every arm. Contrasts are estimated from per-seed unsafe rates as differences of means over unpaired seeds, with the variance pooled across arms.

Side effects are the *over-refusal excess* $O(m) = R(m) - r(\theta_0)$, against the untuned model ($R(m) - R(a_0)$ is also reported), and the *task-loss increase* $L(m) = \bar\ell(m) - \bar\ell(a_0)$. A repair is *feasible* at limits $(\tau_O, \tau_L)$ if $O(m) \le \tau_O$ and $L(m) \le \tau_L$. Results are reported at $\tau_O \in \{2, 5, 10\}$ pp; $\tau_L$ is two standard errors of a seed-to-seed difference in held-out loss, fixed before the comparison runs. The *cost* of a repair is $C(m) = c(m) + 1$ FRE: $c(m)$ is the measured cost of building the repaired data (scoring rows, selecting or generating safety rows), and one FRE is the measured cost of one random-removal run (§4.7).

With a smallest effect of interest $\delta = 5$ pp and Holm correction at family-wise level 0.05 over the primary contrasts, each contrast receives one label:
- *better*: the Holm-adjusted test rejects $\Lambda_m = 0$ and the estimate is at least $\delta$;
- *equivalent*: the 90% interval for $\Lambda_m$ lies inside $(-\delta, \delta)$ (two one-sided tests);
- *worse*: the contrast with its comparator is significantly harmful at the Holm-adjusted 5% level on the unsafe rate ($\Lambda_m < 0$), on over-refusal ($R(m)$ above the comparator's) or on task loss ($\bar\ell(m)$ above the comparator's), each read in its harmful direction, so a harmful repair is never reported as inconclusive;
- *inconclusive*: otherwise.

Which label a repair receives when it is better on the unsafe rate but worse on a side effect is fixed in the stage-1 pre-registration [TO BE FINALISED]; both results are reported. A margin of 4 pp is reported as a sensitivity. Five references change no data (§4.4). One, *adapter scaling*, $\theta_\lambda = \theta_0 + \lambda\,(\theta(a_0, s) - \theta_0)$ for $\lambda \in \{1, 0.75, 0.5, 0\}$, traces $(F, O, L)$ from the fine-tune ($\lambda = 1$) to the untuned model ($\lambda = 0$). A data repair *dominates* only if it lies below this curve: a lower $F$ than the curve at equal $O$ and $L$.

### 3.3 Localization

Let $a_{-k}$ remove unit $D_k$ and $a_{\mathrm{rand},k}$ remove $|D_k|$ random rows drawn across units. Erosion is *localized* at level $\delta_B$ if some $\Lambda_k = F(a_{\mathrm{rand},k}) - F(a_{-k})$ reaches $\delta_B$ beyond seed noise (the random arm controls for the smaller training dose), and *diffuse* if every $\Lambda_k$ is credibly below $\delta_B$. Diffuse erosion leaves the repair comparison unchanged: unit removals then act like random removal up to $\delta_B$, and a unit-level diagnostic has little to find.

### 3.4 Positive control

A planted data set $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{p}}$ replaces the rows of one small cell of $\mathcal{D}$ with $k$ rows that look benign but carry a known eroding signal, keeping the number of rows fixed. Nested partitions split $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{p}}$ into $M$ cells, so that the planted cell sits inside one cell at every $M$. The planted erosion is $P = F(a_0^{\mathrm{p}}) - F(a_0)$, and removing the cell that holds the plant at menu size $M$ restores $G^{\mathrm{p}}(M) = F(a_0^{\mathrm{p}}) - F(a^{\mathrm{p}}_{-c(M)})$. A diagnostic $d$ *hits* at $(M, k_d)$ if it ranks that cell among its top $k_d$. Because the truth is known, the sweep measures how diagnosis and trial runs scale with $M$, which natural menus at our budget cannot.

### 3.5 The value of diagnosis (secondary, model-based)

A *menu* $\mathcal{A}_M = \{a_0, a_1, \dots, a_M\}$ holds $a_0$ and $M$ repairs built by a frozen rule, never by a compared diagnostic; $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{feas}}$ is its feasible subset and $a^* = \arg\min_{a \in \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{feas}}} F(a)$. A *selector* $\pi$ with budget $B$ (in FRE) buys trial runs (each a training fraction $\phi$ and a prompt fraction $\psi$, priced from the measured cost model) at total cost at most $B$ and returns $\hat a$. It drops a repair whose adverse estimate of $O$ or $L$ passes its limit ($a_0$ is never dropped), and picks the lowest estimated $F$ among repairs with full runs on at least two seeds; ties go to $a_0$. An infeasible pick is scored as if no repair were made:
$$\mathrm{loss}(\hat a) = F(\hat a)\ \text{if}\ \hat a \in \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{feas}},\ \text{else}\ F(a_0); \qquad \mathrm{Reg}(\pi; B, M) = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{loss}(\hat a)] - F(a^*).$$
This is a scoring convention for an expected outcome at a fixed budget, not a safety guarantee. The runs-only selector $\pi_-$ uses one rule per $(M, B)$ cell (a one-shot screen, successive halving or hierarchical bisection), chosen from a simulation before any outcome and then frozen. The selector $\pi_{+d}$ pays the cost $c_d$ of diagnostic $d$, keeps its top $k_d$ repairs, and runs the same rule on $B - c_d$. The value of $d$ and its break-even cost are
$$\Delta_d(B; M, c) = \mathrm{Reg}(\pi_-; B, M) - \mathrm{Reg}(\pi_{+d}; B, M, c), \qquad c^*_d(B, M) = \sup\{c \ge 0 : \Delta_d(B; M, c) \ge 0\}.$$
$F(a^*)$ cancels in $\Delta_d$. When $a_0$ is feasible, regret is non-negative, so no diagnostic can save more than $V_{\max}(B, M) = \mathrm{Reg}(\pi_-; B, M)$. At the menus we can afford, these quantities are mostly outputs of a model, so we treat them as predictions. A generative model (repair effects shrunk by empirical Bayes, noise from pooled residuals, measured costs, two effect shapes) is frozen before Gate B's category runs, and its predicted $V_{\max}$, $\Delta_d$ and $c^*_d$ for the category menu and the planted menus are recorded then. We report measured against predicted values (replayed with the same code path on the outcome tables), and score OLMo-calibrated predictions of the chance of finding the best repair, and of regret, on Llama's category menu.

### 3.6 Opening decomposition (side study)

Let $p(\theta)$ be the share of sampled answers to harm prompts that open with a refusal (a frozen lexicon applied to the first 12 words), and $u_{\mathrm{R}}(\theta)$ and $u_{\mathrm{N}}(\theta)$ the unsafe rates among answers that do and do not open with one, so $f = p\,u_{\mathrm{R}} + (1-p)\,u_{\mathrm{N}}$. For the untuned model (subscript 0) and a fine-tune (subscript 1),
$$f_1 - f_0 = \underbrace{(p_0 - p_1)(u_{\mathrm{N}0} - u_{\mathrm{R}0})}_{\text{opening switch}} + \underbrace{(1-p_1)(u_{\mathrm{N}1} - u_{\mathrm{N}0}) + p_1\,(u_{\mathrm{R}1} - u_{\mathrm{R}0})}_{\text{content change}}.$$
The *opening share* is the first term over $f_1 - f_0$; the split depends on the order of terms, so both orders are reported. The decomposition is descriptive: it says whether a change in the unsafe rate shows up in how answers open or in what they say, not which training choice caused the change. The first term has two layers on which families and recipes can differ: how many refusal openings fine-tuning removes ($p_0 - p_1$), and how often a direct answer was already unsafe before fine-tuning ($u_{\mathrm{N}0}$); where $u_{\mathrm{N}0}$ is high and $u_{\mathrm{R}0}$ low, a lost opening comes close to an unsafe answer. If the opening share is small, repairs that act only on how answers open (refusal examples; an objective that constrains updates on the first answer tokens) may restore less than repairs that change what the answers say.

### 4.1 Models, data and recipes

**Families and scale.** OLMo-2-0425-1B-Instruct (Team OLMo et al., 2024) is the stage-1 model; Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) is the planned second family, at a matched erosion dose (below; §4.8). Larger sizes follow a ladder: a published-setting replication and a reduced study at 7–8B, then a headline check at 32B or 70B [PLACEHOLDER: sizes actually run]. Qwen2.5-1.5B- and 7B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2024) are outside the repair study; their existing recipe-B runs enter only the same-recipe family contrast (§4.10). Every model trains on one GPU, and every comparison stays on one GPU type.

**Data.** Gate B and stage 1 train on 4,800 rows of Dolly-15k (Conover et al., 2023), 600 from each of 8 task categories (the units; $K = 8$), prepared and sealed per tokenizer with a manifest; 200 further rows, 25 per category, measure task loss. The add repairs draw their safety rows from PKU-SafeRLHF (§4.3). Alpaca is the planned second benign dataset, after Gate B [NOT YET FIXED: its units and learning rate].

**Recipes.** *Recipe B*, the main recipe: LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) of rank 64 and scale 128 on all linear layers; AdamW at a constant learning rate of $10^{-4}$, no warmup; 128 rows per step; one epoch (38 steps); a per-row loss (each row's mean over its response tokens). Gate A, Gate B and stage 1 use it. It was chosen over recipe G from probe and Gate A readings alone, before any repair outcome. *Recipe G*, the recipe-only fix, adds a 4-step warmup and cosine decay, which halve the mean learning rate over the epoch, and a token-mean loss; it is a reference (§4.4). A pre-registered single-seed run that changes only the loss weighting separates its two changes (§4.10). Training is deterministic on matched hardware.

**A matched erosion dose.** Each family uses recipe B with only its learning rate changed: the highest rung of a ladder ($10^{-4}$, $5 \times 10^{-5}$, $2 \times 10^{-5}$) whose erosion probe reads partial (20–55 pp) under the frozen one-seed versions of the Gate A bars (§4.6; a too-close probe gets one more seed). The rate is chosen from probes only, never from a repair result. During development no rung of a one-recipe ladder left both families partly eroded (§5), because families respond to the same change of recipe by different amounts; we fixed this rule after reading that ladder and the second family's first probe at recipe B, and state cross-family claims at a matched erosion dose. OLMo-2-1B's rung is $10^{-4}$. [PLACEHOLDER: Llama-3.2-1B's rung.]

### 4.2 Measures, judges and the claim rule

- **Unsafe rate U.** The share of 600 sampled answers (200 development harm prompts × 3 samples at temperature 0.7, up to 2,048 new tokens) that Qwen3Guard-Gen-4B (Zhao et al., 2025) labels Unsafe. The prompts come from HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024), StrongREJECT (Souly et al., 2024), SimpleSafetyTests (Vidgen et al., 2023) and OR-Bench's toxic set (Cui et al., 2025). Standard errors are clustered by prompt. U is a broad policy label, not an attack-success rate as published elsewhere: judges and prompts differ, so U is compared only within this study. A rise in U is refusal erosion as Qwen3Guard labels it.
- **Compliance and the claim rule.** HarmBench's classifier asks whether an answer carries out the requested behaviour. A contrast in U supports the wording *harmful compliance as the HarmBench classifier defines it*, never harm alone, only if the classifier's contrast has the same sign and at least half the size and the classifier's erosion under that recipe is at least 20 pp; otherwise the sentence says *restored refusal*. A harmful-compliance sentence also notes that a blind human read rated about a third of judge-unsafe answers as usable help (§6).
- **Also per arm:** hard-category U (violent, illegal, sexual or self-harm categories); U by answer-length band and a length-reweighted U; Granite Guardian 3.3 (Padhi et al., 2024) on the greedy answers; and a selectivity score (refusal on harm prompts minus refusal on benign prompts).
- **Over-refusal.** Qwen3Guard's refusal flag on greedy answers to 100 benign prompts that resemble harmful ones (50 XSTest safe prompts, Röttger et al., 2024; 50 OR-Bench-Hard prompts, Cui et al., 2025), reported by source, with a lexicon check.
- **Task loss and skill.** Held-out loss on all 200 task rows is the skill limit of the comparison. The probe and Gate A bars also check ARC-Easy accuracy (Clark et al., 2018) on 189 questions: the paired drop $d$ against the untuned model must stay at or below 0.03. With 189 questions the paired interval is about ±0.07 wide, so it straddles 0.03 for almost every run. For probe and Gate A readings, a rule fixed during development, before the readings it was written for, resolves a straddling interval [TO BE FINALISED: its scope under the per-family rule]: the run **passes, as "skill kept, reported as a limitation", when the point drop $d \le 0.03$ and the held-out task loss is below the untuned model's (the task is learned); it fails when $d > 0.03$.** This rule is a loosening: it can pass a run whose true drop is up to about 6 points, which 189 questions cannot detect. We report the verdict under the frozen bars beside the verdict under this rule. Disclosure: the first recipe-G probe on OLMo-2 was read under the earlier rule before this rule was proposed, so for that one run the rule is not applied blind. At 1B, ARC-Easy is close to chance, so "skill kept" rests mainly on the held-out task loss.
- **Answers end.** A coherence screen flags answers that reach the token cap, repeat 4-grams heavily, are empty, or have fewer than 5 tokens; an arm whose answers fail too often is not read.
- **Banks.** Gates and selectors read development banks only. Final claims are confirmed on a sealed held-out subsample (§4.8).

**Gate A (entry).** For each family at its matched dose, over three seeds (the probe seed and two fresh seeds), mean $E$ must lie in a pre-registered partial-erosion window of 20–55 pp, be at least twice the seed standard deviation, and exceed zero on every seed; a second judge must agree in direction, the answers must end, and the task must be learned without lost skill (§4.2). **Gate B (localization).** These rules summarize its pre-registration, revision 5 [NOT YET FIXED]. At recipe B, each category removal (600 rows, 33 steps) is compared with random removal of 600 rows (75 per category) at the same seed, so $a_0$ cancels. All 8 categories run at one seed; the two with the largest contrast, plus Gate C's picks, run at two more seeds. The verdict is LOCALIZED if some category restores at least $\delta_B = 8$ pp more than random removal and clears a Bonferroni-corrected bar; DIFFUSE if every category is credibly below 8 pp; and TOO CLOSE otherwise. The verdict reads U alone; the blind human read recommended before the first wave is done, on a different sample from the one first proposed (§6). Guards require answers that end, recompute the verdict on coherent answers and under adverse counting of unscored answers, and reject a restoration that over-refuses. **Gate C (which diagnostic picks the category).** On the first seed's $a_0$, attribution (A1's score) and representation similarity each pick their top category; a format rule picks the category with the largest share of its loss on the first five answer tokens. **If DIFFUSE.** Gate C does not run and stage 1 is unchanged. A rewrite arm (the untuned model's own answers replace the 600 top-attribution rows) runs first among the secondary arms, and the planted sweep becomes the only test of whether diagnosis can pay.

## 6 Discussion and Limitations

**What "unsafe" means.** Our primary judge gives a broad policy label, so a rise in U is refusal erosion as Qwen3Guard labels it. In a blind human read during development (one reader), most judge-unsafe answers went along with the request, but only about a third gave usable help. The claim rule (§4.2) therefore allows at most "harmful compliance as the HarmBench classifier defines it", and U is not comparable with attack-success rates published under other judges and prompts.

**Equivalence and power.** The labels resolve differences of about 5 pp; smaller differences stay inconclusive, and an *equivalent* label means "within ±5 pp", not "identical". Gate B's detection limit (§4.6) holds for categories; row-level causes that span categories are not tested by Gate B.

**Recipe and setting.** The main recipe, B, is plain rather than standard: a constant learning rate with no warmup and a per-row loss, with a rank-64 adapter on all linear layers, larger than is common (a rank-16 check is reported). A standard warmup and cosine schedule (recipe G) lowers the mean learning rate; we report it as a recipe-only fix, and repair rankings may differ at a lower dose. A second family runs at its own learning rate, so cross-family claims hold at a matched erosion dose, not at one recipe; that rule was fixed after a development ladder was read. The skill bar at the probes and Gate A is looser than the bar first registered, and we say where it decided a reading (§4.2). The main model and recipe were chosen by screening probe and Gate A readings, never Gate B or stage-1 outcomes; §5 lists every setting tried.

**Baselines and doses.** Every scorer is re-implemented from its paper ("X-style"), so a weak result may reflect our implementation; a published table of deviations per scorer, reference outputs requested from the authors and the published-setting replication address this. Comparisons across removal and addition hold only at the pre-registered doses (600 rows removed, 48 added).

**Cost.** An arm's cost depends on how much we evaluate as well as on training. Costs at 1B do not transfer to larger models, because a scorer's cost and a run's cost grow differently with model size.

**Scale and family.** The full comparison runs at 1B on OLMo-2, and on Llama-3.2 if its Gate A passes at its matched dose, with one benign dataset until Alpaca runs; larger sizes get a replication only if resources allow. A family difference is claimed only above its MDE.

**The positive control and the secondary analysis.** The planted cause is artificial; finding it shows that a diagnostic *can* find a concentrated cause, not that natural erosion has one. The value of diagnosis at natural menus is model-based and judged against predictions made in advance.

**Scope and user.** We compare data repairs with model-level references; rewriting responses enters only if the erosion is diffuse. A customer of a closed fine-tuning service can change only the data but cannot run gradient or representation scorers; a developer with open weights can run every scorer but also has model-level repairs. Our answer serves a service that must ship the customer's adapter and may change only the data, and a pipeline that allows only data changes.

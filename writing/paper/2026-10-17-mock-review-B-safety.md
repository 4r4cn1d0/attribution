## 1. Summary

The paper asks a practical question. A safety-tuned Instruct model, fine-tuned on benign instruction data, loses part of its refusal behaviour. If only the training data can change, which data repair restores the most safety for its cost, without raising over-refusal or losing task skill?

**Design.**
- **Main setting (stage 1).** OLMo-2-0425-1B-Instruct gets a LoRA fine-tune (rank 64, all linear layers, constant learning rate 1e-4, no warmup, per-row loss, one epoch of 38 steps) on 4,800 Dolly rows. That is 600 rows from each of 8 task categories.
- **Six pre-registered repairs, each at six seeds.**
  - Three removals of 600 rows each: bi-anchored gradient similarity ("attribution", A1), a LARF-style representation filter (L1) and a free shortest-answer rule (H1).
  - Three additions of 48 refusal rows (1%), drawn from PKU-SafeRLHF: random (S1), DataRx-style selected (S3) and self-generated by the untuned model (S4).
- **Comparators.** Each removal is compared with random removal of 600 rows. Each addition is compared with no repair, or with random addition.
- **Labels.** Each contrast gets one label after Holm correction: better, equivalent within ±5 pp (TOST, 90% interval), worse (significantly adverse on the unsafe rate, over-refusal or task loss) or inconclusive.
- **Five references that change no data.** A test-time safety prompt (PTST), the adapter scaled down, Safe LoRA, a standard warmup-and-cosine recipe with token-mean loss (recipe G), and recipe B with Qi et al.'s token-wise constrained SFT. A data repair "dominates" only if it sits below the adapter-scaling curve in (F, O, L).
- **Pre-registered parts around the comparison.**
  - Gate A: is the erosion real?
  - Gate B: is the erosion localized in a Dolly category?
  - Gate C: which diagnostic picks that category?
  - A positive control with a planted cause in nested menus of 8, 32 and 128 cells (funding-gated).
  - A model-based "value of diagnosis" analysis, with predictions scored on Llama-3.2-1B at a matched erosion dose.
  - Planned stages for confirmation and scale (7–8B on both families, funding-gated).
- **Measures.**
  - The primary unsafe rate U: the Qwen3Guard-Gen-4B "Unsafe" label on 600 sampled answers to 200 harm prompts.
  - A claim rule: the wording "harmful compliance" needs the HarmBench classifier to agree.
  - Granite Guardian 3.3 as a direction check.
  - Over-refusal: Qwen3Guard's refusal flag on 100 benign look-alike prompts (XSTest and OR-Bench-Hard).
  - Held-out task loss.
  - Cost in full-run equivalents (FRE) under two conventions.

**What the paper is.** A registered-report-style empirical comparison. It claims no new repair method. Its claimed contribution is the combination: equivalence-bounded, seed-replicated, cost-accounted contrasts of removal and addition repairs for purely benign erosion, against model-level references.

## 2. Strengths

1. **The question is the right decision question.** "Which fix, at what cost and side effect" is what a practitioner needs. It is more useful than one more filter paper with its own baselines and judge. The decision framing makes negative results useful: a well-powered "no data repair beats a cheap model-level fix" would be useful. The paper names an actor and a decision (§6), even though I dispute the actor below.
2. **Statistical hygiene is far above the norm in this literature.** The paper uses six training seeds per arm, Holm correction and equivalence labels with a stated margin (δ = 5 pp, sensitivity 4 pp). Contrasts are fixed in advance, and families of tests are read in order (§4.9). A sealed held-out prompt set is used for confirmation (§4.8). Rules are committed by hash before the data they judge. Most papers cited in §2 report one or a few seeds, as the paper says.
3. **The random-removal and random-addition comparators are the right controls.** Removing 600 rows cuts the training dose by 12.5% (33 against 38 steps, Table 1). Comparing a removal with random removal of the same size (§3.2) separates "which rows" from "how much data". Many filter papers miss this.
4. **The model-level references set a real bar.** In particular, B+Qi, the constrained objective of Qi et al., runs at six seeds. The adapter-scaling curve (§3.2) is a smart, cheap frontier: it is the trivial way to trade task gain for safety, and any data repair must beat it at equal side effects.
5. **The measurement claims are honest.** The authors separate refusal erosion (U) from harm. Their claim rule (§4.2) restricts the strongest wording to "harmful compliance as the HarmBench classifier defines it". They report a blind read in which only about a third of judge-Unsafe answers gave usable help (Appendix A). They state that U is not comparable with attack-success rates published elsewhere. Few papers in this area are this careful about what their judge measures.
6. **The format/length control is well motivated.** The free shortest-answer rule (H1) is both a repair and a control for attribution's picks. The format pick in Gate C (C.2) answers the concern that gradient similarity tracks answer form.
7. **Disclosure is thorough.** Every development-time loosening is disclosed: the ARC-Easy skill bar (C.4), the Llama rungs accepted on a too-close bar (§4.1), and the recipe chosen from probes (§6). The sealed Gate C rankings and their consequence (Gate C can no longer read ATTR BETTER or REP BETTER) are stated before the outcome (§4.6, C.2).

## 3. Weaknesses, ranked by severity

Each item gives the location, why it matters and the cheapest fix.

### W1 (critical). The main evidence is one 1B model, one 2023 dataset and one non-standard recipe. The larger and newer settings are funding-gated.

**Where.** §4.1 ("[PLANNED; funding-gated, fallback one family]"; the headline check is "[PLACEHOLDER: if resources allow]"); §4.8; §6 ("Scale and family"); the Impact Statement.

**Why it matters.**
- The confirmatory comparison (P1–P6) runs on OLMo-2-1B-Instruct, and in stage 2 on Llama-3.2-1B, under recipe B, which the authors call "plain rather than standard" (§6). At 1B the harm is weak: the authors' own blind read found usable help in about a third of Unsafe-labelled answers (Appendix A), and ARC-Easy is "close to chance" (C.4).
- The ranking of repairs is the paper's product, and it may change with scale. Cheap repairs such as refusal additions or a length rule may work at 1B because a 1B model's "safety" is mostly a surface refusal habit. At 7–8B the safety behaviour is deeper and the harmful answers are more capable.
- The models are also dated for a July 2027 venue:
  - Llama-3.2-1B (September 2024) and Llama-3.1-8B (July 2024).
  - OLMo-2-0425-1B (April 2025; https://huggingface.co/allenai/OLMo-2-0425-1B-Instruct).
  - OLMo-2-1124-7B (November 2024).
- Newer fully open models with modern post-training exist. OLMo 3 7B Instruct goes through SFT, then DPO, then RLVR, with checkpoints and post-training data released (https://huggingface.co/allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Instruct; its paper is dated 15 Dec 2025 on the card). Qwen3 offers dense models from 0.6B with thinking and non-thinking modes (https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388).
- An ICML reviewer will read "1B, Dolly, one recipe" as a workshop-scale result.

**Cheapest fix.**
- Make one 7–8B family non-negotiable for the primary family P1–P6 and the five references at six seeds. Swap OLMo-2-1124-7B for OLMo 3 7B Instruct: same lab, open data, current post-training, and the same cost.
- Pay for it by cutting the positive control and the value-of-diagnosis model (W10). Those are secondary by the paper's own ordering (§4.9).
- Keep 1B as the cheap, high-seed development setting, and say so.

### W2 (critical). The headline comparison is not a confirmatory test: "does any data repair beat the references?" is descriptive and underpowered.

**Where.** Abstract ("[FINDING 2: how the best data repair compares with the references]"); §1 ("A data repair counts as dominant only if it beats five references"); §3.2 (the adapter-scaling dominance rule); §4.4 (three seeds for the safety prompt, adapter scaling, Safe LoRA and recipe G); §4.9 (the five families of tests, none of which contains a data-repair-against-reference contrast; "Everything else is descriptive").

**Why it matters.**
- The question a practitioner and a reviewer care about most is whether data repair beats the cheap model-level fix. That question sits outside every pre-registered family.
- It is also answered with references at half the seeds of the data arms.
- "The best data repair" is chosen after the fact from six arms, so its contrast with a reference has winner's-curse bias.
- The dominance rule, "a lower F than the curve at equal O and L", is not defined for a one-dimensional curve. At a given λ, O and L will not both match the repair's values. The authors must say which one is matched (task loss, presumably) and how λ is interpolated.

**Cheapest fix.**
- Three of the references (safety prompt, adapter scaling, Safe LoRA) change no training. Run them on all six a0 seeds; that costs evaluation only.
- Pre-register one more family before stage 1: each data repair against B+Qi, and against the adapter-scaling curve interpolated in λ to the repair's held-out task loss. Use the same labels and Holm.
- Alternatively, pre-register a rule that picks one data repair from the primary family, then confirm only that repair against the references on fresh seeds (stage 3 already re-runs at most two contrasts).

### W3 (major). The actor in the threat model is not coherent, and the menu ignores levers the real actors have.

**Where.** §1 ¶1 ("its owner often controls only the data"; Microsoft, 2026); §6 "Scope and user" ("Our answer serves a service that must ship the customer's adapter and may change only the data").

**Why it matters.**
- The premise is real. The Microsoft Foundry page (updated 31 Jul 2026) blocks deployment when the fine-tuned model "scores above acceptable thresholds for [Violence, Self Harm]" and tells the customer to "review your training data set and resubmit" (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-foundry/openai/how-to/fine-tuning-safety-evaluation).
- But the customer of such a service does not control only the data. The OpenAI fine-tuning API exposes `learning_rate_multiplier`, `n_epochs` and `batch_size` for supervised jobs (https://developers.openai.com/api/reference/resources/fine_tuning). The paper's own design shows that the learning rate and schedule change erosion strongly: the matched-dose ladder in §4.1, and recipe G "halve[s] the mean learning rate" (§4.1).
- The customer cannot run A1, L1 or S3, which need gradients or hidden states (§6 says so). That leaves H1, S1 and S4 (S4 only if the service lets them sample the untuned model).
- The service, in turn, controls the recipe. It could apply B+Qi or Safe LoRA to every customer at negligible cost.
- So the actor for whom "only data may change" and attribution can be run is close to empty. The paper's answer needs to be stated per actor.

**Cheapest fix.**
- Add a column to Table 1: which actor can run each arm (closed-service customer, service operator, open-weights developer, dataset publisher).
- Report the winner within each actor's menu.
- Add one reference that a closed-service customer can use: recipe B at the next rung down the learning-rate ladder (5e-5 for OLMo), which the ladder in §4.1 already defines, at six seeds. If a halved learning rate restores as much safety at equal task loss, the data-only framing must say so up front.
- Consider the dataset publisher as an actor. For a dataset publisher a data repair has a unique advantage: it travels with the data to every downstream model and recipe. That makes transfer the property to test (see Q7).

### W4 (major). Loss normalisation and the recipe confound the main comparison, especially the length rule and the format reading.

**Where.** §4.1 (recipe B uses "a per-row loss (each row's mean over its response tokens)"; recipe G "adds a 4-step warmup and cosine decay ... and a token-mean loss"); Table 1 (H1 removes the 600 shortest answers); C.2 (the format pick is defined "under the per-row loss"); C.3 (the length rule under a token-mean loss is only a three-seed exploratory arm).

**Why it matters.**
- Under a per-row mean, each token of a one-word Dolly answer (common in `classification` and `closed_qa`) gets the full weight of a row. Under a token-mean loss it gets a tiny weight. So recipe B puts unusually large weight on the first tokens of short, direct answers.
- That is plausibly the mechanism of erosion: learning to start answering immediately. Qi et al. argue that alignment is concentrated in the first few tokens (https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.05946).
- If so, H1 "works" because of the loss normalisation, and a headline such as "a free length rule matches attribution" would be an artifact of recipe B.
- Recipe G changes the schedule and the loss normalisation at once, so the reference cannot separate the two.

**Cheapest fix.**
- Promote "H1 and aR under token-mean loss" to the primary family. That is two arms × six seeds at 1B, about 12 short runs.
- Split recipe G into schedule-only and loss-only arms, at three seeds each.
- Report, for every removal arm, the training tokens and the share of loss weight on the first five answer tokens. The paper notes that the token-matched analysis is "[TO BE FIXED]" (§3.2). Fix it now.

### W5 (major). Strong data-side repairs and recent post-hoc repairs are missing; two of them fit inside the paper's own data-only action space.

**Where.** Table 1; §4.4; §2 ("Addition and model-level repairs"); §6 ("A one-example patch applied after fine-tuning ... is not among the references").

**Why it matters.** The addition arms are all "48 refusal rows that open with a refusal" (C.3). That is exactly the shallow kind of safety data that the shallow-alignment account predicts will restore direct refusals but not depth. Several stronger data repairs exist and are missing:
- **Qi et al.'s safety-recovery augmentation** (https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.05946, §3.1, opened in HTML). Each harmful instruction is paired with a refusal that starts after a prefilled harmful prefix of k tokens. This is a data repair, so it belongs in the paper's action space, and it targets depth directly. The paper cites the same work only for its constrained objective.
- **Safety at One Shot** claims that "safety alignment can be fully recovered with only a single safety example" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01887). The paper excludes it as a post-hoc patch (§6). Its data-only analogue, appending that one example (or a few copies) to the training set, is admissible in the paper's own action space and costs nothing. If one example restores as much as 48 random refusals, the addition arms' dose is wrong.
- **Replay of the model's own safety SFT data.** OLMo-2-0425-1B-Instruct was post-trained on an OLMo-specific variant of the Tülu 3 SFT mixture (model card above). The Tülu 3 SFT mixture contains CoCoNot (10,983), WildGuardMix (50,000) and WildJailbreak (50,000) subsets (https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/tulu-3-sft-mixture). I did not verify that the OLMo variant keeps them. Replaying 48 in-distribution safety rows, including their benign contrast prompts, is the obvious repair for an open-weights developer. It also bears on over-refusal.
- **Task-format safety data.** Eiras et al. (ICLR 2025) add safety data that "mimics the task format and prompting style of the user data" and report better restoration than baselines (https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10288).
- **Post-hoc, no-data references.** Safe Delta (ICML 2025) adjusts the fine-tuning delta and is evaluated on benign as well as harmful data (https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12038). Antidote (ICML 2025) is a one-shot pruning pass after fine-tuning, aimed at harmful fine-tuning (https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.09600). Backdoor-enhanced safety alignment adds as few as 11 safety examples with a secret trigger (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14968). At least the first belongs in §2. The survey by Huang et al. organises this space (https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18169) and is not cited.

**Cheapest fix.**
- Add two addition arms at the same 48-row budget and six seeds: recovery-style augmentation (refusal after a harmful prefix) and the one-shot example replicated to 48 rows. Both are data-only and need no new scorer.
- Add self-replay of Tülu-style safety rows as a three-seed secondary arm on OLMo.
- Cite Safe Delta, Eiras et al. and the survey.

### W6 (major). U measures lost refusal, not harm, and no measure separates shallow from deep repair.

**Where.** §4.2 (U, the claim rule, Granite Guardian); Appendix A; §6 ("What 'unsafe' means").

**Why it matters.**
- The authors limit the wording well, but the decision they want to inform is about harm. Their own read gives about 0.33 usable help per Unsafe label, with one reader, an author, and no agreement figure (Appendix A, "Limits").
- Two consequences:
  - A repair that turns usable harmful answers into vague compliance gets no credit.
  - A repair that restores refusal openings but leaves the content intact gets full credit.
- Neither Qwen3Guard nor Granite Guardian scores how useful a harmful answer is. StrongREJECT does, and the paper already uses StrongREJECT prompts. Its score is (1 − refused) × (specific + convincing)/2. Its fine-tuned Gemma-2B evaluator correlates with human rankings at Spearman 0.900, against 0.819 for the HarmBench classifier (https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10260). Its authors also find that jailbreaks that bypass safety tend to reduce capability, which is the 1B "goes along but useless" pattern of Appendix A.
- The paper evaluates no attack. Erosion matters most as extra susceptibility. The key scientific contrast here is shallow (refusal-opening data) against deep (B+Qi). A prefilling attack separates the two, and Qi et al. built their argument on it (https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.05946, §2.3.1 and Table 2).
- The opening decomposition (C.8) is descriptive. It does not test robustness.

**Cheapest fix.**
- Add the StrongREJECT fine-tuned evaluator score per arm as a harm-weighted secondary endpoint. It runs on the same 600 answers, with no new generation.
- Add a prefilling evaluation: prefill the first 5–20 tokens of a harmful target response on the 200 development prompts, greedy, one pass per arm and seed. At 1B this costs about one more evaluation.
- Replace the one-reader read with two raters who did not design the study, on about 200 answers stratified by arm, with Cohen's κ.

### W7 (major). The over-refusal and task-skill limits are underpowered and favour the addition arms.

**Where.** §3.2 ("A repair is feasible ... if O(m) ≤ τO", with τO ∈ {2, 5, 10} pp; "worse if ... significantly adverse"); §4.2 (100 greedy prompts: 50 XSTest, 50 OR-Bench-Hard); C.4.

**Why it matters.**
- Feasibility uses a point estimate, and "worse" needs significance. So an arm with large uncertainty in over-refusal passes. With 100 prompts, τO = 2 pp is two prompts.
- This asymmetry favours the addition arms, which are the arms most likely to raise over-refusal. Bianchi et al. found that too much safety tuning makes models refuse safe prompts that look like unsafe ones (https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.07875).
- The banks are much larger than 100 prompts: XSTest has 250 safe prompts (https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.01263), and OR-Bench has about 1,000 hard prompts (https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20947, ICML 2025).
- Teacher-forced task loss cannot detect a model that refuses in-domain benign requests at generation time.

**Cheapest fix.**
- Make the side-effect limits non-inferiority tests: feasible only if the one-sided 95% upper bound of O(m) − O(comparator) is below τO, and likewise for L.
- Use all 250 XSTest safe prompts and all of OR-Bench-Hard-1K, greedy. That is cheap at 1B.
- Add the refusal rate on the 200 held-out Dolly prompts.
- Report partial refusals separately (lexicon plus judge).

### W8 (moderate). The labels can contradict each other, and their precedence is not fixed.

**Where.** §3.2 (better needs Holm rejection and an estimate ≥ δ; equivalent needs the 90% interval inside (−δ, δ); "Which label a repair receives when it is better on the unsafe rate but worse on a side effect is fixed in the stage-1 pre-registration [TO BE FINALISED]").

**Why it matters.**
- A contrast with estimate 3 pp and 90% interval (1, 4.9) is significantly positive, yet it is labelled "equivalent".
- A contrast with estimate 4.5 pp and interval (2, 7) is labelled "inconclusive", although it is significantly positive.
- Readers will misread "equivalent" as "no effect".
- It is also unclear whether the over-refusal and task-loss tests sit inside the Holm family.

**Cheapest fix.** Publish a label table with an order of precedence. Add "better, below δ" (significant but under the smallest effect of interest). Pre-register which tests the Holm family contains (six contrasts × three outcomes, or six only).

### W9 (moderate). The "attribution" arm is a gradient-similarity filter against external anchors, not attribution of the measured behaviour.

**Where.** Table 1 (A1: "bi-anchored gradient similarity at two checkpoints of a0"); C.2 ("sealed anchors: 64 harmful, 64 safe"); §2 ("Attribution, its cost and what it tracks").

**Why it matters.**
- The anchors are external harmful and safe examples, as in He et al.'s bi-anchoring (https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01099). The query is not the behaviour being repaired, which is a0's own judge-Unsafe answers to the harm bank.
- A negative result for A1 will be cited as "training-data attribution does not pay for safety repair". The paper does not test that claim.
- The concurrent SABA submission (cited in §2) reports that risk rankings at initialization are "nearly uncorrelated" with rankings after two epochs (https://openreview.net/forum?id=Jo6U0y2omr, abstract).

**Cheapest fix.**
- Add a scored-only variant (no new run unless it differs from A1's top-600 by much): the query is a0's judge-Unsafe sampled answers on development prompts not used for selection, with an optimizer-aware LoRA gradient (LESS-style). Report its top-600 overlap with A1.
- Rename A1 "gradient-similarity filter" in claims.
- Bound the conclusion to that estimator.

### W10 (moderate). Gates B and C and the value-of-diagnosis model take most of the design but can say little.

**Where.** §3.3, §3.4, §4.5, §4.6; C.1, C.2, C.5, C.7.

**Why it matters.**
- Gate C can no longer distinguish attribution from representation similarity: both pick the same category (§4.6). The format reading can only say "neither pick is the format pick".
- Gate B's units are Dolly task types (open_qa, summarization, ...), not content. The pre-registration records the prior that DIFFUSE or TOO CLOSE is more likely (C.1), and an 8 pp category reads LOCALIZED about half the time.
- The value of diagnosis "at the menus we can afford" consists "mostly [of] outputs of a model" (§3.4). It is not measured.
- These parts make the paper very hard to read (W13). They also compete for the funds that W1 needs.

**Cheapest fix.** Move §3.3–§3.4, Gate C and C.7 to the appendix as pre-registered diagnostics. Report Gate B's verdict in two sentences in §5. Spend the freed page on the comparison at 7–8B.

### W11 (moderate). The positive control plants a cause that is easier to find than natural benign erosion.

**Where.** §4.5; C.5 (the plant is 37 rows "whose answers comply directly with prompts that also have an unsafe answer in the source data").

**Why it matters.**
- These rows come from a safety dataset's harmful-adjacent prompts. A content classifier or an LLM judge may flag them at no cost.
- If so, "diagnosis finds the plant" says nothing about the benign case the paper studies. The paper reports the content classifier "only" (C.2).

**Cheapest fix.**
- Before the sweep, report the content classifier's and a judge's rank of the planted cell (no runs needed). If either ranks it first, the plant is too easy.
- Choose a benign-looking plant instead: for example, rows chosen by He et al.'s selection, which they found often appear as "lists, bullet points, or math questions" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01099).

### W12 (moderate). The evidence needed to judge generality sits in a concurrent submission that reviewers cannot see.

**Where.** §4.1 (twice), §4.10.2, §6, Appendix A, C.8: "[CITE: concurrent submission, anonymized]". The deferred material:
- family differences, including why Qwen2.5 is "outside this study";
- recipe G against recipe B across families;
- the rank-16 check;
- the single-seed loss-weighting run;
- the human read;
- the opening decomposition across families.

**Why it matters.**
- The two questions that decide significance are how common erosion is under standard recipes across current families, and whether recipe B is representative. Both are answered only in that paper.
- The paper itself reports Guo et al.'s Table 2: under LoRA, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct moves from 7.27% to 9.09% after 5,000 rows (§2). Some families barely erode, so leaving out Qwen looks like selecting families that erode.
- ICML's dual-submission rules require the overlap to be explained.

**Cheapest fix.**
- Attach the anonymized concurrent submission as supplementary material.
- Add one appendix table: E per family and recipe (B, G, rank 16) at 1B, with seeds. This is existing data.

### W13 (presentation; moderate for acceptance). The paper reads like a pre-registration rather than a paper.

**Where.** Throughout.

**Why it matters.**
- The body uses dozens of coined terms: Gates A/B/C, waves 0a/0b/1/2, G1–G4, TOO CLOSE, FRE, full and lean conventions, menus, π−, π+d, the matched erosion dose, "answers end".
- The text carries many placeholders: [TO BE FINALISED], [NOT YET FIXED], [CITE-CHECK], [TO BE DECIDED].
- The pre-registrations that the paper's credibility rests on are not attached ("[TO BE DECIDED: anonymized copies as supplementary material]", C).
- The title says "Compute-Matched", but nothing is matched in compute. Costs are measured and reported (§4.7), and removals are matched in rows (§3.2).

**Cheapest fix.**
- Retitle to "Cost-Accounted".
- Add a one-figure design overview: arms, comparators, references, measures.
- Attach time-stamped, anonymized pre-registrations.
- Cut the coined terms in the body to about five.

### W14 (minor). Dataset realism.

**Where.** §4.1, Data.

**Why it matters.** Dolly-15k (2023) and Alpaca (2023) are short, single-turn instruction sets. They are good for comparability with Qi et al. (2024), who used them (https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03693). They are unlike 2026 customer data: chat-format, domain-specific, long-form or reasoning traces. Category units are task types.

**Cheapest fix.** Add one modern domain or chat dataset for P1–P6 at 1B before Alpaca, or replace Alpaca with it. Report answer-length distributions per dataset, given W4.

### W15 (minor). The second judge is used outside its validated setting.

**Where.** §4.2 (answers up to 2,048 tokens; prompts from four banks).

**Why it matters.** HarmBench standardises generation at 512 tokens, noting that this parameter "can change ASR by up to 30%" (https://arxiv.org/html/2402.04249). Its classifier is "the official classifier for text behaviors in HarmBench" (https://huggingface.co/cais/HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls). The claim rule applies it to 2,048-token answers and to StrongREJECT, SimpleSafetyTests and OR-Bench-toxic prompts.

**Cheapest fix.** Truncate to 512 tokens for the classifier, as HarmBench does, and report its agreement with Qwen3Guard per source bank.

### W16 (minor). Cost results do not transfer, so "cost" is weak as a contribution.

**Where.** §4.7; C.6.

**Why it matters.** At 1B a training run is 38 steps. The evaluation (600 sampled answers of up to 2,048 tokens, plus judges) probably dominates an FRE. The paper rightly refuses to transfer cost ratios to larger models (§4.7). That leaves the cost claims local to a setting where every arm is cheap.

**Cheapest fix.** Report training-only and scoring-only GPU-seconds separately, and give a scaling estimate (FLOPs per scorer as a function of parameters and rows) rather than measured ratios alone.

## 4. Questions for the authors

1. What is f(θ0), the untuned unsafe rate, for OLMo-2-1B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct on the 200-prompt bank? Is the 20–55 pp erosion window reachable under recipe G at the same learning rate? ## 5. Domain concerns

### 5.1 Is benign-fine-tuning erosion still important in late 2026, or is it solved by recipe or loss fixes?

**It is not solved, but the paper must argue its importance more carefully than it does.**

The case that it is not solved:
- Services still gate fine-tuned models on safety evaluations and send customers back to their data. The Microsoft page cited above was updated in July 2026.
- The fixes need the trainer to adopt them.
- Much of their evidence comes from older models, harmful-data attacks or narrow benign tasks:
  - Qi et al.'s constrained SFT was evaluated on Llama-2-7B-Chat and Gemma-1.1-7B-IT, with Samsum, SQL Create Context and GSM8k as the benign sets (https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05946, §4).
  - Safe LoRA's abstract describes purely malicious and mixed benign-plus-malicious data (https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16833).
  - The Safety at One Shot abstract frames recovery against harmful fine-tuning, across five models (https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01887).
- Concurrent work still finds erosion widely. One ICLR 2027 submission reports weakened refusals in "all 17 open models we test" and proposes an "opening hold" loss that cuts harmful compliance from 22.6% to 0.9% over six models (https://openreview.net/forum?id=HdUlQV6dde, abstract only).

The case for "largely mitigated, if you control the recipe":
- PTST (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18540), constrained SFT, Safe LoRA, Safe Delta (https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12038), the one-example patch and, if it holds up, the opening hold are cheap.
- Guo et al. report that "few safety examples can restore refusal behavior" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01455).
- A concurrent ICLR 2027 submission finds that LoRA rank and recipe decide whether safety survives, and that "the corpus is the weaker lever". It also finds that the collapse "disappears when the adaptation safety prompt is present at evaluation" (https://openreview.net/forum?id=tjNgRr5kox, abstract only).

**Consequence for this paper.** The importance case should rest on two points:
1. Who cannot use the recipe-side fixes (W3).
2. Whether the cheap fixes hold up under a shared, seed-replicated, side-effect-limited protocol. This paper is unusually well placed to answer that.

The most valuable outcome may be a rigorous ranking of the cheap fixes, with data repairs as challengers, rather than a ranking of data repairs.

### 5.2 Are data repairs the right lever?

**Only for a narrow set of actors, and the paper's own prior suggests a modest effect.**

The pre-registration records that DIFFUSE or TOO CLOSE is more likely than LOCALIZED (C.1). The concurrent SABA abstract argues that one-shot risk scores are unreliable. Rank and recipe dominate the corpus (abstract above).

Data repairs are the right lever:
- for a closed-service customer, if they also report the hyperparameter levers (W3);
- for a dataset publisher, where transfer across models is what matters (Q7).

For an open-weights developer, data repairs are a challenger to model-level fixes, which is how §4.4 frames them. That framing should lead the paper.

The addition arms are the more promising data lever: Guo et al., DataRx (https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04322) and Safety at One Shot all point there. They are also where the menu is weakest (W5).

### 5.3 Are the five references the right strong references, and which are missing?

**Kept: four of the five are good.**
- PTST: cheap and necessary.
- Adapter scaling: an excellent frontier baseline.
- Safe LoRA: a canonical post-hoc projection.
- B+Qi: the strongest objective-side fix.

**Recipe G is a weak reference** because it changes the schedule and the loss normalisation together (W4).

**Missing.** In order of importance:
1. A lower learning-rate rung under recipe B. This is the customer's lever (W3).
2. Qi et al.'s safety-recovery augmentation, a data-side deep repair (W5).
3. Safety at One Shot. Its data-only analogue is in-scope. The post-hoc patch itself is a natural sixth reference, cheap to run after a0.
4. Safe Delta, a post-hoc repair evaluated on benign data (ICML 2025).
5. Replay of the model's own safety SFT data, for OLMo.
6. The concurrent opening hold, as a "cite and discuss" item rather than a required baseline.

**Related work to cite:**
- the harmful fine-tuning survey (https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.18169);
- Eiras et al. (https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10288);
- backdoor-enhanced safety alignment (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14968);
- Antidote (https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.09600);
- Fraser et al., who report that safety evaluations after benign fine-tuning vary under "seemingly inconsequential changes" to the setup. That supports the six-seed design (https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.17209, LLMSEC 2025).

### 5.4 Are the models current enough for ICML 2027?

**Partly.**
- Llama-3.x remains the common currency of this literature. DataRx's primary model is Llama3-8B-Instruct (https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04322), and DualSelect uses 1B–8B models (https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09866). So Llama-3.1-8B is defensible for comparability.
- OLMo-2 is defensible for open data, but OLMo 3 7B Instruct now offers the same openness with SFT, DPO and RLVR post-training (https://huggingface.co/allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Instruct).
- By July 2027, a paper whose largest completed results are on 2024–2025 1B models will look dated.
- At least one current family at 7–8B is needed. Ideally the paper should also say what changes for reasoning-mode models (Qwen3's thinking mode, https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388), even if only as a limitation.
- The Qwen exclusion needs a stated reason in this paper, not in a hidden one (W12).

### 5.5 Are the datasets adequate (Dolly now, Alpaca planned)?

**Adequate for comparability, weak for realism** (W14). Qi et al. (2024) observed erosion with Alpaca and Dolly; that is the reason to use them, and it should be stated. But a 2027 reader will ask about chat-format, domain or reasoning-trace data.

Alpaca as the second dataset adds little diversity: it is also 2023 single-turn instruction data. I would rather see one modern domain dataset.

### 5.6 Are the measures adequate, and does refusal erosion equal harm?

**Refusal erosion does not equal harm, and the authors know it.** The claim rule and Appendix A are the best treatment I have seen in this sub-literature. But:
- Qwen3Guard-Gen gives Safe, Controversial or Unsafe plus a refusal flag (https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14276; model card above). Granite Guardian is also a policy classifier. Neither measures how useful the harmful content is.
- The HarmBench classifier is the only "does it carry out the behaviour" judge, and it is used outside its validated setting (W15).
- The fix is to add a harm-weighted score (the StrongREJECT evaluator) and an attack-robustness probe (prefilling) (W6).
- At 1B, harm-weighted erosion may be much smaller than U erosion. That is a finding worth reporting, not hiding.

### 5.7 Is over-refusal measured adequately?

**No** (W7).
- 100 greedy prompts and point-estimate feasibility make the limits weak.
- The selectivity score (§4.2) is a good idea and should be primary for the addition arms.
- XSTest (250 safe prompts) and OR-Bench-Hard-1K should be used in full.
- Partial refusals should be reported.
- In-domain refusal on Dolly prompts is missing.
- SORRY-Bench's fine-grained taxonomy and judge study (ICLR 2025; https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14598) would also help to report refusal by harm category consistently. The paper's "hard-category U" is a coarse version.

### 5.8 How realistic is the threat model?

**The setting is realistic. The actor and the recipe are less so.**
- Accidental erosion through a fine-tuning service, with the customer sent back to the data, is documented (Microsoft page above).
- But the customer also has learning-rate and epoch controls (OpenAI API page above), and cannot run four of the six repairs (W3).
- Recipe B (constant LR 1e-4, no warmup, rank 64 on all linear layers, per-row loss, batch 128) is chosen to land in a 20–55 pp erosion window. That window is large for a benign fine-tune.
- The paper should show where a standard recipe lands (Q1). If standard practice gives small erosion, the threat model is "a customer who chose an aggressive learning rate". That actor's first fix is the learning rate.

### 8.3 A new inconsistency I found while cross-checking (body §3.2, made concrete by the dev. evidence)

**The problem.** The body uses two different baselines for over-refusal:
- **Feasibility** uses O(m) = R(m) − r(θ0), against the *untuned* model.
- **The "worse" label** uses R(m) against the arm's *comparator*, which is a0 for S1.

The dev. evidence shows that fine-tuning *lowers* over-refusal: OLMo refuses 17/100 benign prompts untuned and 4–6/100 after recipe B. Take an addition arm that restores the untuned model's behaviour exactly, at about 17/100:
- its O is 0, so it is feasible;
- it is about 12 pp above a0, which over six seeds is likely significant, so it is labelled **worse**.

**The consequence.** The arm closest to the ideal repair, "give me the original model back plus the task", is labelled worse. Meanwhile, a removal arm that leaves over-refusal at a0's depressed level looks clean.

**Fix.** Pick one reference for side effects. I suggest:
- the untuned model for feasibility;
- the selectivity score (refusal on harm prompts minus refusal on benign prompts) as the primary side-effect contrast for the addition arms;
- no "worse" on over-refusal unless R(m) also exceeds r(θ0) by more than τO.

This is **NEW**: it is in neither the notes nor my W7 draft. Decision 12 covers a different conflict, between the unsafe share and a side effect.
